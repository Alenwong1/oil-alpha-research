Additional inputs come from the already-cached release-specific EIA table 1, restricted to the 744 previously retained releases. Read current-week gasoline and distillate product supplied, validating the observation date and cached file checksum against the retained release ledger. Product supplied is an apparent-demand proxy, not measured final consumption. Construct four-week means from four consecutive weekly observations (5–9-day gaps only). Construct product days of supply as stocks in million barrels divided by mean product supplied in thousand barrels/day, multiplied by 1000. These are our constructed measures, not a claim to reproduce the official published days-of-supply series exactly. No current-history fallback.

Scarcity state uses the preceding retained release's crude stocks and constructed gasoline/distillate days of supply, with a maximum 10-day observation gap. Standardize against strictly earlier releases in a fixed ±4 seasonal-week window over the last five years, at least 104 observations overall and 12 seasonal observations. Positive scarcity means lower stocks/coverage than its seasonal history. Add the three scarcity states and their interactions with the corresponding bullish inventory surprise (negative model residual). Clip standardized values at ±4 and interactions at ±8. This explicitly represents starting inventories available before the latest release.

Demand confirmation adds seasonal z-scores of the current four-week gasoline/distillate product-supplied proxies and three interactions: bullish crude surprise × refinery-input z; average bullish product surprise × average demand z; bullish crude surprise × import z. These test distinctions between draws accompanying refinery/demand activity versus lower imports, not causal identification of each draw's cause.
